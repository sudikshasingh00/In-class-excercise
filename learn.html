Learn About Camelids

Alpacas and llamas are South American camelids, domesticated thousands of years ago in the Andes. They are related to camels, which is easier to believe once you have watched one lie down.


Alpacas

Smaller, bred for fibre, and happiest in a group. An adult alpaca stands about three feet at the shoulder. There are two types: huacaya, whose crimped fleece stands out from the body, and suri, whose fleece hangs in long locks. Most of our herd are alpacas, and a good number of them are elderly.


Llamas

Larger, bred as pack animals, and far more independent. An adult llama can reach six feet at the ear tips and carry a quarter of its own weight. Their confidence is why llamas are used to guard other livestock, and why ours are the ones who walk out front on a trek.


Living With Age

An older camelid is a different animal to care for. Teeth wear down, weight becomes harder to hold, joints stiffen, and feed has to be adjusted animal by animal. Much of what we know we have learned from this herd, and we would rather pass it on than have someone else learn it the slow way.

Understand Camelid Behaviour

An ethogram is a catalogue of the behaviours a species actually performs, written down so that an observer records what they see rather than what they assume. This is ours, and it is the basis of every visit we run.

Sounds

    Humming — the everyday sound. Curiosity, contentment or mild worry; context tells you which.
    Clucking — a soft clicking, usually a mother to her cria or a greeting between familiar animals.
    Orgling — the male breeding call. Unmistakable, and not a sound you will hear here.
    Alarm call — a rising shriek that puts the whole herd on its feet. Usually a dog, sometimes a deer, occasionally a plastic bag.

Postures

    Cush — lying down with the legs folded underneath. A resting animal, or a refusing one.
    Pronking — bouncing on all four legs at once. Play, and only play.
    Tail flagging — tail raised over the back. Alert, and deciding what to do about it.
    Ears back and flat — the clearest warning you will get. Give the animal room.
    Neck wrestling — two animals pushing neck against neck to settle rank. Normal, noisy, and not a fight.

Spitting

Camelids spit mostly at each other, over food and over rank, and rarely at people who have not earned it. A spit comes with a warning first: ears flat, head up, air pushed hard through the nose. The animal is telling you something. Step back, and it ends there.
