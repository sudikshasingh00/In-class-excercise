Our History

Dusty Toes began with a llama. Dusty is eighteen, the matriarch of our trekking team, and the animal who made it clear that this work needed to be more than a few people helping out on weekends.

We are currently focusing on a herd of sixty llamas and alpacas in the Lanark Highlands. Their longtime owners are getting older and can no longer provide the standard of welfare they would want, and that is where we come in. We help with the daily care, the training and the programming, while the animals stay in the home they know. The owners take part as much as they are able. It is a collaboration.

Often when owners reach this moment in their lives, the decision to rehome is emotionally difficult — and it is not what is best for them or for the animals. This is the herd’s home, and they have made friends within it. Rehoming would split them up. And where would they go?

We are working toward incorporating as a nonprofit, so we can care for this herd sustainably, for the rest of their lives.

Our Mission

Dusty Toes exists to provide care and education for llamas and alpacas. We provide daily care, medical attention and enrichment for a herd of South American camelids, many of them elderly or with special needs. Alongside their care, we share what we learn — about camelid behaviour, welfare, and the quiet good that comes from time spent with these gentle animals.

Caring for the herd comes first, but it is not all we imagine. As we grow, we hope to share these animals more widely: as teachers of camelid behaviour and welfare, and as partners in programs that connect people and animals for the good of both. That is the sanctuary we are building toward. Maybe a sanctuary is not a place at all, but a movement.

Meet Our Team

Dusty Toes is a small, dedicated team: the farmers whose home this is, the person who leads the animals’ daily care, a board taking shape around us, and the volunteers who show up week after week.

The Farm Owners

Keith and Elizabeth Adam own Serendipity Farm & Sanctuary in the Lanark Highlands, where every animal we care for lives. They have kept alpacas and llamas here for many years, and over that time the farm has grown into a sanctuary of thirty-three alpacas and twenty-five llamas, open to visitors by appointment for treks, private tours and a farm gift shop whose proceeds go straight back into the animals’ care.

Dusty Toes exists because of a decision Keith and Elizabeth made: that the herd should stay together, in the place it knows, for the rest of the animals’ lives. We work alongside them, not instead of them.

The Camelid Spa Team

We do shearing differently.

Shearing is normally a stressful experience for the animals. Professional shearers depend on speed — it is their livelihood, and they need to get through as many animals as they can, as quickly as they can. That often means llamas are shorn lying down, and the elderly and special-needs animals do not get the care they deserve.

That is where we come in. We shear and trim at a gentler pace, using hand clippers when the situation calls for it and electric shears otherwise. Llamas are shorn standing, supported through the whole process with a soft touch, soothing music and quiet conversation. Afterwards there are treats, and a gentle massage for anyone who wants one.

We leave the young, healthy alpacas to the professionals. We are glad to take the elderly alpaca who needs a softer touch.

Meet the Herd

We share the care of roughly sixty animals — around thirty-three alpacas and twenty-seven llamas, ranging from youngsters to elders well into their twenties. A good number are seniors in our care for the rest of their days, which is exactly how we like it: no more breeding, no revolving door, just a herd growing older together.

Dusty

Eighteen years old, matriarch of the trekking team, and the llama who inspired this nonprofit. She sets the pace on every walk and the rest of the herd falls in behind her. If you have met one animal here, it is almost certainly Dusty.

Perseus

One of our alpacas with a health story to tell. He needs more veterinary attention than most of the herd, and we are working hard to keep him comfortable and thriving. Much of what we have learned about caring for an ageing alpaca, we learned from him.

Noel

At seven months, the youngest of the herd, and proof that a sanctuary is as much about beginnings as endings. He was born here and has never known anywhere else.

Chewy and Her Family

Noel’s mother, Chewpaca — Chewy to everyone here — arrived in May 2025 from the Waterloo Park zoo, and she did not come alone. Her family came with her:

    Anne, an alpaca
    Dolly, a llama
    Timo, a llama
    Domino, a llama

They arrived as a group and they have stayed one. Keeping families together is the whole reason this herd is not being rehomed animal by animal.
